Film, then data, then security, then photography. One discipline in four sets of clothes.

I studied film and digital media at Baylor and finished in 2015. Film school teaches you something it doesn't advertise on the brochure. You are handed raw material, hours of footage and a scattered set of images and a story that doesn't yet exist, and your job is to organize it so that someone else can see what you saw. Composition. Sequence. What to leave in the frame and what to cut. The whole craft is the management of attention.

I finished an MBA in cybersecurity at Baylor in 2024. I expected security to feel like a departure. It felt like coming home. Security is the management of attention too. A threat on a network is invisible until someone makes it visible: surfaces the pattern, names the anomaly, shows the thing before it does its damage. That is the same act as cutting a scene. You are deciding what someone needs to see, and when.

For the last two years I have run enterprise operations for a satellite communications company. I cannot name the systems or the customers, and I won't. The discipline is the point: incident command when something breaks and the clock is real, forecasting tools I built from scratch because the ones we needed didn't exist, operational analytics in an environment where downtime is not an abstraction. You are handed more raw material than any person can hold, and your job is to find the shape in it and make the shape legible to someone who has to act.

Data engineering is the most literal version of this. You take a mess of logs and exports and half-broken spreadsheets, the residue of systems that were never meant to talk to each other, and you organize it until a decision becomes obvious. The work is not the dashboard. The work is the noticing that has to happen before the dashboard means anything.

And then there is the camera, which is where I started and where I keep returning. I photograph landscapes and the natural world: clouds the size of weather systems, the water on a single leaf after rain. A thunderhead and a rain-wet leaf carry the same weight, because scale was never the subject. Attention is. Photography is the purest form of the discipline, because there is no production, no pipeline, no incident to command. There is only the thing that is already there, and whether or not you noticed it.

Noticing is the whole practice, and I'll be plain about the word. Most of what matters is already in front of us. The work is learning to see it. I believe that about networks and I believe it about people, and the same sentence covers both.

I am a person of faith, and I'll say that plainly too, because it isn't decoration on the rest. It's the floor. I believe in service: in showing up when it's hard, in doing the work in front of you without waiting for applause. Eagle Scout taught me the shape of that before I had words for it.

Take the raw material. Find the shape. Make it visible, so someone else can see it too and, if you've done it right, act on it before the moment passes.

The rest is learning to pay closer attention.
