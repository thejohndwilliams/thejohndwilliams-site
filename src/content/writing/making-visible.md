People ask how a film degree leads to security work. They expect a story
about a pivot, a clean break, a moment I left one thing for another. There
was not one. I never left the core discipline. I kept finding it wearing
different clothes.

I studied film and digital media at Baylor and finished in 2015. Film school
teaches you something it does not advertise on the brochure: how to take raw
material, hours of footage, a scattered set of images, a story that does not
yet exist, and organize it so that someone else can see what you saw.
Composition. Sequence. What to leave in the frame and what to cut. The whole
craft is the management of attention.

An MBA in cybersecurity followed in 2024, back at Baylor. I expected the
security work to feel like a departure. It felt like coming home. Security is
the management of attention too. A threat on a network is invisible until
someone makes it visible: surfaces the pattern, names the anomaly, shows the
thing before it does its damage. That is the same act as cutting a scene. You
are deciding what someone needs to see, and when.

Since early 2025 I have run enterprise operations for a satellite
communications company. I cannot name the systems or the customers, and I
will not. The discipline is the point: incident command when something breaks
and the clock is real, forecasting tools built from scratch because the ones
we needed did not exist, operational analytics in an environment where
downtime is not an abstraction. None of that looks like film. All of it is
the same problem. You are handed more raw material than any person can hold,
and your job is to find the shape in it and make the shape legible to someone
who has to act.

Data engineering is the most literal version of this. You take a mess: logs,
exports, half-broken spreadsheets, the residue of systems that were never
meant to talk to each other. You organize it until a decision becomes
obvious. The work is not the dashboard. The work is the noticing that has to
happen before the dashboard means anything.

Photography is where the discipline goes when nobody is paying for it. A
landscape does not arrive composed. The light does something for a few
minutes, the weather assembles and disbands, and the work is to be standing
there, attending, when the frame happens. Then the same edit as always: what
stays, what goes, what order the frames belong in so a stranger can stand
where I stood.

Making visible. It is one job. I have only ever had the one.
